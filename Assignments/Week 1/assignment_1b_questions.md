# Assignment 1B Written Questions

## 1. Why do whole-share constraints prevent perfect rebalancing?

Whole-share constraints prevent perfect rebalancing because the exact dollar amount required for a target allocation usually does not divide evenly by a security's share price. For example, if a portfolio should have $250 invested in a stock trading at $180 per share, I cannot invest exactly $250 using only whole shares. I would have to buy either one share or two shares, which would leave the position either below or above its target weight. As a result, some tracking error remains even after the portfolio is rebalanced.

## 2. Why might a portfolio manager deliberately not rebalance exactly to target?

A portfolio manager may choose not to rebalance exactly to target because trading can create costs and other disadvantages. Small differences from the target allocation may not justify paying transaction costs, creating taxable gains, increasing turnover, or causing unnecessary market impact. A manager may also expect the position to move back toward its target naturally or may want to maintain additional cash for liquidity. For these reasons, it can be more efficient to allow small deviations from the target rather than trading every time a portfolio becomes slightly out of balance.

## 3. What additional constraints do institutional portfolios face?

Institutional portfolios can face many additional constraints beyond target weights. These may include liquidity limits, transaction costs, taxes, minimum trade sizes, market impact, compliance rules, concentration limits, client-specific restrictions, cash requirements, settlement timing, and restrictions on certain securities or industries. Large portfolios also have to consider whether a trade can be completed without significantly affecting the market price. These constraints mean that the mathematically ideal portfolio may not always be the most practical or appropriate portfolio to implement.
